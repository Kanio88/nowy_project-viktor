Rethinking ADHD Support

Sensory-driven strategies for focus, regulation, and daily living

Introduction

ADHD is widely misunderstood. One of the most persistent myths is that people with ADHD “lack motivation.” As your newsletter explains: “ADHD is not a motivation problem. It is a regulation problem.” This distinction changes everything about how we support individuals in daily living.

ADHD as a Regulation Disorder

Regulation refers to the nervous system’s ability to manage arousal, stay calm, stay alert, and transition between states. For people with ADHD, this system is more reactive, more sensitive, and more dependent on interest and novelty.

  • Cognitive regulation: Working memory, planning, task switching.
  • Emotional regulation: Intense emotions, frustration sensitivity, rejection sensitivity.
  • Sensory regulation: Noise, clutter, textures, smells can overwhelm and block functioning.
“I don't lack motivation. I lack the ability to generate it on demand for things my brain doesn't find meaningful.”

The Role of Sensory Input in Focus

Many people assume distractions are the enemy of focus. For ADHD, the opposite is often true. Movement, background noise, tactile input, and proprioception help regulate the nervous system and support sustained attention.

  • Movement: Fidget tools, standing desks, walking while thinking.
  • Soundscapes: Brown noise, lo-fi music, ambient sound.
  • Proprioception: Weighted pads, wobble cushions, pressure-based tools.
  • Lighting: Cool bright light for focus; warm dim light for transitions.

Executive Function Scaffolding

Effective support reduces cognitive load rather than adding to it. Externalising tasks helps compensate for working memory challenges.

  • Visual schedules
  • Micro-tasks
  • External timers
  • Environmental cues
  • Body doubling

Hyperfocus and Flow States

Hyperfocus is not a flaw — it is a powerful ADHD strength. But transitions out of hyperfocus can be difficult. Timers, gradual cues, and interest-based framing help support smoother transitions.

ADHD-Friendly Home Environments

Small environmental changes can dramatically reduce sensory overload and improve task initiation.

  • Visual organisation: Open shelving, transparent containers.
  • Sensory zoning: Calm corners, focused work areas.
  • Environmental triggers: Preparing the next task in advance.
  • Transition rituals: Music, scents, movement.

Co-Regulation in Practice

Co-regulation is one of the most powerful tools in ADHD support. A calm, attuned support worker helps the individual return to baseline during dysregulation.

“The most helpful thing my support worker does is just sit with me while I start things.”
